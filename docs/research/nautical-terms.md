## Nautical Directions and Positions

### Basic Directional Terms

**Bow**
- The front of a boat
- Forward refers to the direction toward the bow

**Stern**
- The back of the boat
- Aft refers to toward or at the stern

**Port**
- The left side of a vessel when facing the bow
- Port and left have the same number of letters (memory aid)
- Marked with red navigation lights
- International convention uses red for port side

**Starboard**
- The right side of a vessel when facing the bow
- Marked with green navigation lights
- International convention uses green for starboard side

**Important Note**: Port and starboard never change, making them unambiguous references that are independent of a mariner's orientation

### Combined Position Terms

**Port Bow**
- The front left side of the boat

**Starboard Bow**
- The front right side of the boat

**Port Quarter**
- The left rear section of the boat

**Starboard Quarter**
- The right rear section of the boat

### Relative Position Terms

**Abeam**
- At right angles to the centerline of the boat
- A relative bearing at 90 degrees (starboard) or 270 degrees (port) as measured clockwise from the ship's bow

**Astern**
- Behind the boat
- To go astern is to drive the boat in reverse
- Moving or positioned toward the rear of a ship

**Beam**
- The maximum breadth (width) of the boat
- A transverse member which supports the deck
- "On the beam" means an object is at right angles to the centerline

### Navigation Direction Terms

**Bearing**
- The direction of an object from an observer, measured in degrees true or magnetic
- The horizontal direction of a line of sight between two objects on the surface of the Earth

**Azimuth**
- A specific type of bearing which measures the direction of an object in relation to true north
- Measured in the horizontal plane, clockwise from 0° to 360°
- Same as absolute bearing

**Heading**
- The direction in which the ship is pointing at any instant
- The compass direction the vessel's bow points
    
**Course**
- The direction in which a vessel is steered or is intended to be steered
- Direction through the water

## Nautical Measurements

### Distance Measurements

**Nautical Mile**
- 1 nautical mile = 1,852 meters
- 1 nautical mile = 1.1508 statute (land) miles
- One nautical mile equals one minute of latitude
- Definition: The distance of one minute of arc along a meridian

**Fathom**
- 1 fathom = 6 feet
- Used primarily for measuring water depth
- 1 nautical mile = 1,013.33 fathoms
- 1 fathom = approximately 0.000164579 nautical miles
- Historical note: Six feet was almost exactly one-thousandth of a nautical mile (6,080 feet)

### Speed Measurements

**Knot**
- Unit of speed equal to one nautical mile per hour
- 1 knot = 1.852 km/h
- 1 knot ≈ 1.151 mph
- 1 knot ≈ 0.514 m/s
- Important: Never say "knots per hour" - a knot already means nautical miles per hour

## Chart and Navigation Terms

### Navigation Aids Overview

**Aids to Navigation (AtoN)**
- Special structures used to enhance safety and provide position references
- Includes buoys, day beacons, lights, lightships, radio beacons, fog signals, and marks
- Provides "street signs" on the water

**Nautical Chart**
- Provides an aerial view of a waterway
- Shows Aids to Navigation, landmarks, and bridges
- Contains information on water depth, latitude and longitude
- Essential for safe navigation

### Types of Navigation Aids

**Buoys**
- Floating objects usually anchored to a specific location on the seafloor
- Drawn at an oblique angle with oblique text on charts
- Standard shapes:
  - Cylindrical (can)
  - Conical (nun)
  - Spherical
  - Pillar
  - Spar

**Beacons**
- Fixed structures attached to the seabed
- Drawn upright on charts
- More permanent than buoys

**Day Beacons**
- Unlighted fixed structures
- Used for daytime navigation

### Navigation Rules for Buoys

**"Red Right Returning"**
- When heading from open water to inland water (or upstream in a river):
  - Keep red markers to your right
  - Keep green markers to your left
- Essential rule for safe channel navigation

### Chart Symbols

**Universal Chart Symbols**
- Seafaring nations agreed in 1982 on a universal set of chart symbols
- Standardized abbreviations, colors, and symbols
- Used in both paper nautical charts and electronic systems
- Each Aid to Navigation has a corresponding chart symbol

### Position Reference Systems

**Latitude**
- Angular distance north or south of the equator
- Measured in degrees, minutes, and seconds
- One minute of latitude equals one nautical mile

**Longitude**
- Angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian
- Measured in degrees, minutes, and seconds
- Lines of longitude converge at the poles

## Radio and Communication Terms

### VHF Radio Basics

**VHF (Very High Frequency)**
- Primary marine communication system
- Channel 16 is the international distress frequency (156.8 MHz)
- Used for ship-to-ship and ship-to-shore communications

### Emergency Signal Hierarchy

#### 1. MAYDAY (pronounced "may-day")
- **Most urgent** distress signal
- Used ONLY when a person or boat is threatened by grave or imminent danger
- Requires immediate assistance
- Life-threatening situations
- Transmitted on VHF Channel 16

**When to Use MAYDAY:**
- Sinking vessel
- Fire onboard
- Medical emergency threatening life
- Any situation with immediate danger to life

#### 2. PAN-PAN (pronounced "pahn-pahn")
- **Urgency signal**
- Safety of vessel or person is at risk
- Does NOT pose immediate danger to life (at the moment)
- Situation could escalate to MAYDAY

**When to Use PAN-PAN:**
- Man overboard (if not immediately life-threatening)
- Boat drifting into shore
- Engine failure in dangerous area
- Medical emergency (serious but not immediately life-threatening)

#### 3. SÉCURITÉ (pronounced "say-cure-i-tay")
- **Safety signal**
- Transmits information about the safety of navigation
- No immediate danger to vessel or persons
- Important navigational or meteorological warnings

**When to Use SÉCURITÉ:**
- Approaching storms
- Navigation light failures
- Submerged logs or debris
- Military gunnery practice
- Other navigational hazards

### Key VHF Channels

**Channel 16 (156.8 MHz)**
- International distress frequency
- Emergency calls
- Initial contact channel
- Must monitor when underway

**Channel 22A**
- Coast Guard announces storm warnings on Channel 16 before broadcasting on 22A
- Weather and safety broadcasts

**MF 2182 kHz**
- Medium frequency distress channel
- Alternative to VHF for longer range

## Safety and Emergency Terms

### Critical Emergency Terms

**Man Overboard (MOB)**
- Any incident where a person falls into the sea from a vessel
- One of the most critical emergencies at sea
- Requires immediate action to maximize survival chances
- MOB devices transmit distress signals on VHF maritime radio frequencies

### Emergency Equipment

**EPIRB (Emergency Position Indicating Radio Beacon)**
- Small electronic device for emergency situations
- Helps search and rescue authorities pinpoint your position
- Continuously sends signal for at least 48 hours once activated
- Operates on 406 MHz radio frequency
- Signals received by Cospas-Sarsat satellite constellation

**PLB (Personal Locator Beacon)**
- VHF-type PLBs often referred to as MOB devices
- Transmit distress signals on VHF maritime radio frequencies
- Personal emergency beacon

### Distress Signals

**International Distress Signal**
- Internationally recognized means for obtaining help
- Used when person, watercraft, aircraft, or vehicle is threatened by serious or imminent danger
- Requires immediate assistance
- MAYDAY signals must ONLY be used where there is grave and imminent danger to life

### Visual Distress Signals

**Flares**
- Visual signaling devices
- Alert nearby vessels or aircraft to your location
- Types:
  - Hand-held flares: burn for three minutes
  - Aerial flares: shot into the air
  - Smoke signals: for daytime use

**Flare Requirements:**
- Life span of 3 years
- Must be replaced before expiration date
- Used to localize or pinpoint exact location

**Other Visual Signals:**
- Orange smoke
- Signal mirrors
- Dye markers
- Flags (international distress flag)

### Emergency Procedures

**Distress Call Protocol:**
1. Transmit on VHF Channel 16 or MF 2182 kHz
2. State appropriate signal (MAYDAY, PAN-PAN, or SÉCURITÉ)
3. Provide vessel information
4. State nature of distress
5. Give position (latitude/longitude or bearing/distance from landmark)
6. Number of persons aboard
7. Description of vessel
8. Any other pertinent information

## Weather and Sea State Terms

### Beaufort Scale

**Overview**
- Empirical measure relating wind speed to observed conditions
- Full name: Beaufort wind force scale
- Devised in 1805 by Francis Beaufort, Royal Navy hydrographer
- Scale ranges from 0 to 12
- Originally related qualitative wind conditions to effects on frigate sails

**Beaufort Scale Values:**

| Force | Wind Speed | Description | Sea Conditions |
|-------|------------|-------------|----------------|
| 0 | <1 knot | Calm | Sea like a mirror |
| 1 | 1-3 knots | Light air | Ripples without crests |
| 2 | 4-6 knots | Light breeze | Small wavelets |
| 3 | 7-10 knots | Gentle breeze | Large wavelets, crests begin to break |
| 4 | 11-16 knots | Moderate breeze | Small waves, frequent white horses |
| 5 | 17-21 knots | Fresh breeze | Moderate waves, many white horses |
| 6 | 22-27 knots | Strong breeze | Large waves, white foam crests |
| 7 | 28-33 knots | Near gale | Sea heaps up, white foam streaks |
| 8 | 34-40 knots | Gale | Moderately high waves, breaking crests |
| 9 | 41-47 knots | Strong gale | High waves, dense foam streaks |
| 10 | 48-55 knots | Storm | Very high waves, sea surface white |
| 11 | 56-63 knots | Violent storm | Exceptionally high waves |
| 12 | 64+ knots | Hurricane | Air filled with foam and spray |

### Sea State

**Definition**
- General condition of the free surface on a large body of water
- Considers wind waves and swell
- Measured at a specific location and moment
- Effect of local winds on sea conditions (independent of traveling swell)

**Douglas Sea Scale**
- Developed in 1920s by Captain H.P. Douglas
- Used by World Meteorological Organization and MetService
- Purpose: estimate roughness of the sea
- Defines sea state based on wave height and conditions

### Wave Types

**Wind Waves (Wind Sea)**
- Generated by local wind
- Shorter wavelength
- Higher frequency than swell
- Characterized by steeper, choppier conditions

**Swell**
- Waves that have traveled from a distant storm or weather system
- Longer wavelengths than wind waves
- Lower frequency than wind waves
- More regular and predictable pattern
- Can travel thousands of miles

### Weather Terms

**Wind Speed**
- Measured in knots for marine purposes
- Affects sea state and sailing conditions
- Critical for safety planning

**Wave Height**
- Vertical distance from trough to crest
- Measured in feet or meters
- Significant wave height: average of highest one-third of waves

**Wave Period**
- Time between successive wave crests
- Measured in seconds
- Longer periods indicate swell; shorter periods indicate wind waves

**Fetch**
- Distance over which wind blows to generate waves
- Longer fetch creates larger waves
- Important for wave forecasting

## Anchoring and Mooring Terms

### Anchoring Overview

**Ground Tackle**
- General name for all equipment needed to anchor a boat
- Components include:
  - Anchor
  - Chain
  - Line (rope)
  - Connecting elements (shackles, swivels)

### Key Anchoring Terms

**Rode**
- The anchor line, including chain
- Can be made of:
  - Rope only
  - Chain only
  - Combination of rope and chain (most common)

**Scope**
- Ratio of the length of rode to the water depth
- Formula: Rode Length ÷ (Water Depth + Freeboard)
- General rule: Let out at least 7 times the rode as the depth of water plus boat's freeboard
- More scope = better holding power
- Minimum safe scope: 5:1 in calm conditions, 7:1 or more in rough weather

**Windlass**
- Mechanical device used to raise and lower the anchor
- Horizontal capstan in the bow
- Can be manual or powered (electric or hydraulic)
- Used for weighing (raising) anchor

**Holding Ground**
- The area of seafloor that holds an anchor
- Quality varies by bottom type:
  - Good holding: mud, clay, sand
  - Poor holding: rock, weed, hard sand
  - Variable: gravel, coral

**Anchor Watch**
- Crewmembers assigned to monitor ship while anchored or moored
- Responsibilities:
  - Ensure anchor is holding
  - Monitor position
  - Watch for dragging
  - Monitor weather changes
  - Watch for other vessels

### Anchor Types and Equipment

**BBB Chain**
- Type of chain generally used for windlasses
- "Blacksmith Bolt and Buckeye" chain
- Standardized for marine use

**Swivel**
- Rotating connector between anchor and rode
- Prevents rode from twisting

**Shackle**
- Metal connector used to join components
- Must be properly sized and secured

### Mooring vs. Anchoring

**Mooring**
- Securing to a permanent mooring buoy or ball
- Pre-installed anchor system
- Pick up mooring line with boat hook
- Usually requires mooring permit or fee

**Anchoring**
- Deploying your own anchor
- Free in most locations (check local regulations)
- More control over position
- Requires more equipment and skill

**Docking**
- Securing vessel to a fixed structure (pier, dock, wharf)
- Uses dock lines and fenders
- Different technique from anchoring or mooring

### Anchoring Procedures

**Setting the Anchor**
1. Choose appropriate location (depth, holding ground, swing room)
2. Approach slowly into wind/current
3. Lower anchor to bottom
4. Back down slowly while paying out rode
5. Set anchor with reverse throttle
6. Check scope and position
7. Take bearings to verify position

**Weighing Anchor**
1. Start windlass or begin manual hauling
2. Motor slowly toward anchor
3. Retrieve rode
4. Break anchor free from bottom
5. Clean anchor and rode
6. Secure anchor for passage

## Rules of the Road at Sea

### COLREGS Overview

**Full Name**
- International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea 1972
- Also known as Collision Regulations (COLREG)
- "Rules of the Road" or navigation rules for vessels at sea

**Authority**
- Published by International Maritime Organization (IMO)
- International maritime law
- Proven breach may lead to prosecution, even without collision

**Purpose**
- Prevent collisions between vessels
- Establish standardized behavior for all mariners
- Define right-of-way situations
- Specify required lights, shapes, and signals

### Structure of COLREGS

**41 Rules Divided into Six Parts:**

**Part A - General**
- Application and definitions
- Responsibility

**Part B - Steering and Sailing**
- Conduct of vessels in any condition of visibility
- Conduct of vessels in sight of one another
- Conduct of vessels in restricted visibility

**Part C - Lights and Shapes**
- Technical specifications
- When and where to display

**Part D - Sound and Light Signals**
- Fog signals
- Maneuvering signals
- Warning signals

**Part E - Exemptions**
- Vessels exempted from certain requirements

**Part F - Verification**
- Compliance verification procedures

**Four Annexes:**
1. Technical requirements for lights and shapes and positioning
2. Sound signaling appliances specifications
3. Additional signals for fishing vessels in close proximity
4. International distress signals

### Key Navigation Lights

**Power-Driven Vessels Underway**
- Masthead light (white, forward)
- Sidelights (red port, green starboard)
- Stern light (white, aft)

**Sailing Vessels Underway**
- Sidelights (red port, green starboard)
- Stern light (white, aft)
- Optional: combined tricolor at masthead

**Vessels Under Oars**
- Must show lights or have flashlight ready
- Should display sidelights if practical

**Fishing Vessels**
- Special lights depending on fishing method
- Red over white when trawling
- Additional signals when operating in close proximity

### Right-of-Way Concepts

**Stand-On Vessel**
- Vessel with right-of-way
- Must maintain course and speed
- Should only maneuver if collision unavoidable

**Give-Way Vessel**
- Vessel required to yield
- Must take early and substantial action
- Must keep clear of stand-on vessel

### Priority of Vessels

**General Hierarchy (least to most maneuverable):**
1. Vessel not under command (highest priority)
2. Vessel restricted in ability to maneuver
3. Vessel constrained by draft
4. Vessel engaged in fishing
5. Sailing vessel
6. Power-driven vessel (must give way to all above)

### Meeting Situations

**Head-On**
- Each vessel alters course to starboard (right)
- Pass port-to-port

**Crossing**
- Vessel with other on starboard side gives way
- Give-way vessel should alter course to pass astern

**Overtaking**
- Overtaking vessel gives way
- Must keep clear until past and clear
- Overtaking applies regardless of vessel type

### Sailing Vessel Right-of-Way

**Between Sailing Vessels:**

1. **When on different tacks:**
   - Vessel on port tack gives way to vessel on starboard tack

2. **When on same tack:**
   - Windward vessel gives way to leeward vessel

3. **Port tack vessel uncertain of other's tack:**
   - Port tack vessel gives way

### Additional Key Rules

**Narrow Channels**
- Keep to starboard side of channel
- Small vessels must not impede large vessels confined to channel
- Crossing vessels must not impede vessels following channel

**Traffic Separation Schemes**
- Follow traffic lanes in designated direction
- Cross at right angles when necessary
- Avoid anchoring in traffic lanes

**Restricted Visibility**
- Reduce speed to safe speed
- Be ready to maneuver
- Use radar if available
- Sound fog signals

**Sound Signals in Restricted Visibility**
- Power-driven making way: one prolonged blast every 2 minutes
- Power-driven stopped: two prolonged blasts every 2 minutes
- Sailing, fishing, restricted maneuverability: one prolonged + two short every 2 minutes

## Sailboat Parts and Rigging

### Major Structural Components

**Mast**
- Long, standing vertical pole holding the sails
- Can be single or multiple (main, mizzen, foremast)
- Made of wood, aluminum, or carbon fiber
- Supported by standing rigging

**Boom**
- Horizontal spar to which a sail's lower edge (foot) is attached
- Pivots at the mast
- Controlled by the mainsheet
- Can swing across boat during jibes

**Keel**
- Large, heavily weighted fin-like structure secured to bottom of boat
- Functions:
  - Keeps boat upright (counteracts heeling)
  - Reduces leeway (sideways drift)
  - Provides directional stability
- Types: fin keel, full keel, wing keel, bilge keels

**Rudder**
- Part of the underwater steering apparatus
- Located near the stern, outside the hull
- Controlled by tiller or wheel
- Turns boat by redirecting water flow

### Rigging Overview

**Definition**
- Equipment used to attach sails and mast to boat
- Consists primarily of various lines (ropes)

**Two Main Categories:**

### 1. Standing Rigging

**Purpose**
- Supports the mast and sail plan
- Generally fixed in position
- Not adjusted during normal sailing

**Components:**
- **Forestay/Headstay**: From bow to mast top, supports mast forward
- **Backstay**: From stern to mast top, supports mast aft
- **Shrouds**: From sides of boat to mast, provide lateral support
- **Spreaders**: Horizontal struts extending from mast to optimize shroud angle

### 2. Running Rigging

**Purpose**
- Used to operate the sails
- Adjusted frequently during sailing
- Controls sail position and shape

**Key Running Rigging Lines:**

**Halyards**
- Haul sails up to set position and hold them there
- Attach to head of sail
- Run through sheave at top or middle of mast
- Return to deck level for operation
- Named by sail: main halyard, jib halyard, spinnaker halyard

**Sheets**
- Control the sails
- Trim sail (tighten in) or ease (loosen and let out)
- Named by sail: mainsheet, jib sheet, spinnaker sheet
- Critical for sail angle and power

**Other Running Rigging:**
- **Outhaul**: Controls foot tension of mainsail
- **Cunningham**: Controls luff tension of mainsail
- **Vang/Kicking strap**: Controls boom height and mainsail twist
- **Topping lift**: Supports boom when sail is lowered
- **Reefing lines**: Reduce sail area in strong winds

### Basic Sail Reference

**Primary Sails:**
- **Mainsail**: Primary sail, attached to mast and boom
- **Jib**: Foresail, attached to forestay
- **Genoa**: Large jib that overlaps mainsail
- **Spinnaker**: Large, lightweight downwind sail

**Note:** See [Sail Components and Types](#sail-components-and-types) for comprehensive sail terminology

### Deck Hardware

**Cleats**
- Secure lines under load
- T-shaped or cam cleats

**Winches**
- Mechanical advantage for trimming lines
- Manual or powered

**Blocks (Pulleys)**
- Change direction of lines
- Provide mechanical advantage

**Traveler**
- Allows athwartships (side-to-side) adjustment of mainsheet
- Controls angle of boom

**Turnbuckles**
- Adjust tension in standing rigging
- Threaded adjustment mechanism

## Sail Components and Types

### Sail Anatomy - Three Corners

**Head**
- Top corner of the sail
- Where halyard attaches
- Highest point when sail is hoisted
- Typically has reinforced grommet or headboard

**Tack**
- Forward lower corner of the sail
- Attachment point at mast (mainsail) or bow (jib)
- Fixed point that doesn't move during trimming
- Named from nautical term for wind direction

**Clew**
- Aft lower corner of the sail
- Where sheets attach
- Primary control point for trimming
- Moves in/out when adjusting sail

### Sail Anatomy - Three Edges

**Luff**
- Leading edge of the sail
- Forward edge that faces the wind first
- Attached to mast (mainsail) or forestay (jib)
- First part to show signs of luffing (flapping)
- Luff tension controls draft position

**Leech**
- Trailing edge of the sail
- Aft edge where wind exits
- Contains leech line for shape control
- Often has telltales for trim indication
- Leech twist affects sail efficiency

**Foot**
- Bottom edge of the sail
- Lower edge between tack and clew
- Attached to boom (mainsail) or free (jib)
- Foot tension affects sail depth

### Sail Construction Components

**Battens**
- Stiffening members inserted into sail
- Run perpendicular from leech toward luff
- Types:
  - Full battens: Run from leech to luff
  - Short battens: Only support leech
  - Compression battens: Push against mast
- Functions:
  - Support leech and prevent flutter
  - Maintain sail shape
  - Extend roach area
  - Improve performance

**Batten Pockets**
- Sleeves sewn into sail to hold battens
- Open at leech end for batten insertion
- Closed or tensioned at luff end

**Reef Points**
- Reinforced grommets in sail for reefing
- Located in rows parallel to boom
- First reef, second reef, third reef (progressively smaller sail area)
- Include tack cringle and clew cringle at each reef level

**Cringles**
- Reinforced metal or rope rings in sail
- Larger and stronger than standard grommets
- Located at:
  - Reef tack positions
  - Reef clew positions
  - Head, tack, and clew corners

**Headboard**
- Reinforced panel at head of sail
- Distributes loads from halyard
- Usually triangular or rectangular
- Contains head attachment point

**Cunningham Eye**
- Grommet located above tack on luff
- Used for cunningham line attachment
- Increases luff tension without raising boom
- Controls draft position in sail

**Clew Outhaul Ring/Grommet**
- Reinforced attachment point at clew
- Connects to outhaul line
- Controls foot tension on mainsail

**Leech Line**
- Light line running inside leech hem
- Adjustable from clew
- Controls leech curl and flutter
- Prevents excessive leech flapping

### Headsail Types (Forward Sails)

**Working Jib**
- Standard jib, doesn't overlap mainsail
- Approximately 100% of foretriangle
- Good all-around performance
- Easier to tack than larger jibs

**Genoa (Jenny)**
- Large overlapping headsail
- Sizes indicated by percentage:
  - 110% Genoa: Moderate overlap
  - 130% Genoa: Standard cruising size
  - 150% Genoa: Large, light air genoa
  - 155-170%: Very large, specialized
- Clew extends aft of mast
- Better performance than working jib
- Requires careful trimming

**Blade Jib (Tall Boy)**
- High-aspect ratio jib
- Tall and narrow shape
- Better pointing ability
- Common on modern racing boats

**Storm Jib**
- Very small, heavily built headsail
- Bright orange color for visibility
- Used in severe weather (Force 8+)
- Typically 5-10% of foretriangle
- Sets on inner forestay if available

**Staysail**
- Small sail set on inner forestay
- Used between mast and jib
- Common on cutters and ketches
- Improves heavy weather performance

**Code Zero (Screecher)**
- Large reaching sail
- Between genoa and spinnaker
- Asymmetric shape
- Used for close reaching
- Furling or free-flying versions

**Drifter (Reacher)**
- Ultra-light genoa for light winds
- Very large, full-cut sail
- Used in winds under 8 knots
- Often bright colors
- Difficult to handle in strengthening winds

### Downwind Sails

**Spinnaker (Kite, Chute)**
- Large, lightweight balloon-shaped sail
- Symmetrical or asymmetric designs
- Set flying (not attached to stay)
- Requires pole or tack line
- Used for reaching and running
- Colors often bright and multicolored

**Symmetric Spinnaker**
- Traditional spinnaker shape
- Both clews identical
- Requires spinnaker pole
- Better for dead downwind
- More complex to set and jibe

**Asymmetric Spinnaker (A-Sail)**
- Unequal luff and leech
- Tacks to bow, no pole required
- Easier to handle than symmetric
- Better for reaching angles
- Can't go as deep downwind

**Gennaker**
- Hybrid between genoa and spinnaker
- Asymmetric design
- Furling or free-flying
- Versatile reaching sail
- Easier handling than spinnaker

### Mainsail Types

**Standard Mainsail**
- Traditional triangular shape
- Attached to mast and boom
- Most common configuration

**Full-Batten Mainsail**
- Battens run full width of sail
- Better shape retention
- Easier to reef and furl
- Common on modern cruising boats
- Quieter when flogging

**Loose-Footed Mainsail**
- Foot not attached to boom
- Only tack and clew connect to boom
- Better sail shape control
- More efficient in some conditions

**Boom-Footed Mainsail**
- Foot attached to boom along full length
- Traditional configuration
- Easier to manage for cruising

**In-Mast Furling Mainsail**
- Rolls into mast for storage
- Convenient for short-handed sailing
- Compromised sail shape
- Reduced performance vs. traditional

**In-Boom Furling Mainsail**
- Rolls into boom for storage
- Better shape than in-mast
- Still convenient for cruising
- Heavier boom required

### Heavy Weather Sails

**Trysail**
- Small, strong mainsail replacement
- Bright orange for visibility
- Sets on separate track or slides
- Used in storm conditions (Force 9+)
- Boom not used (free-footed)
- Typically 15-20% of mainsail area

**Storm Jib**
- Minimum headsail for heavy weather
- Reinforced construction
- High-visibility color

**Storm Staysail**
- Small inner staysail
- Used with storm jib or alone
- Improves balance in heavy weather

### Sail Materials

**Dacron (Polyester)**
- Most common cruising sail material
- Durable and UV resistant
- Stretches over time
- Economical
- Good all-around performance

**Laminate Sails**
- Multiple layers bonded together
- Less stretch than Dacron
- Better shape retention
- More expensive
- Less durable in UV
- Types: Pentex, Spectra, Dyneema, Carbon

**Mylar/Film Sails**
- Clear or tinted plastic film
- Very low stretch
- Excellent shape
- Susceptible to UV and fatigue damage
- Racing applications

**Kevlar**
- High-strength aramid fiber
- Extremely low stretch
- Excellent performance
- Poor UV resistance (turns brown)
- Expensive
- Racing and performance cruising

**Carbon Fiber**
- Ultimate performance material
- Minimal stretch
- Lightweight
- Very expensive
- Racing applications
- Poor UV resistance

## Sail Trim and Shape Control

### Primary Sail Controls

**Sheets**
- Control sail angle to wind
- Primary power control
- In = more power and pointing
- Out = less power, bearing away
- Mainsheet: Controls mainsail
- Jib sheets: Control headsail (port and starboard)

**Halyard**
- Hoists sail to proper position
- Controls luff tension when sailing
- Tighter = moves draft forward
- Looser = moves draft aft
- Critical for sail shape optimization

**Outhaul**
- Controls foot tension on mainsail
- Adjusts sail depth (fullness)
- Tight outhaul = flatter sail (strong wind)
- Loose outhaul = fuller sail (light wind)
- Affects lower sail section only

**Cunningham**
- Controls luff tension
- Alternative to halyard for draft control
- Pulls down on luff from cunningham eye
- Tighter = moves draft forward
- Used when halyard is at maximum
- More effective than halyard in some designs

**Boom Vang (Kicking Strap)**
- Controls boom height and twist
- Pulls down on boom
- Prevents boom from rising
- Critical for downwind sailing
- Tight vang = less twist, more power
- Loose vang = more twist, less power

**Traveler**
- Controls boom position athwartships
- Adjusts sail angle without changing sheet
- Up (windward) = more power upwind
- Down (leeward) = depowers in gusts
- Independent from sheet trim

**Backstay**
- Tensions forestay and bends mast
- Tighter = flatter mainsail, tighter forestay
- Looser = fuller mainsail, slightly sagging forestay
- Critical for sail shape in varying winds
- More tension in stronger winds

**Backstay Adjuster**
- Mechanism for adjusting backstay tension
- Hydraulic, mechanical, or manual
- Allows quick tension changes

### Sail Shape Terminology

**Draft (Camber)**
- Maximum depth of curve in sail
- Measured as percentage of chord
- Typical range: 10-15% for upwind
- Deeper draft = more power
- Flatter draft = less power, better in strong wind

**Draft Position**
- Location of maximum depth in sail
- Measured from luff
- Typical range: 40-50% back from luff
- Forward draft = better pointing
- Aft draft = more power, harder to point
- Controlled by luff and foot tension

**Twist**
- Change in sail angle from bottom to top
- Natural twist allows for wind shear
- Too much twist = lost power aloft
- Too little twist = stalled upper sail
- Controlled by:
  - Sheet tension (main and jib)
  - Boom vang (main only)
  - Traveler position (main only)

**Entry**
- Shape of sail near luff
- Fine entry = less curve at luff
- Full entry = more curve at luff
- Affects pointing ability and power

**Exit**
- Shape of sail near leech
- Open leech = twisted, power shed
- Closed leech = powerful, risks stall
- Controlled by sheet and vang

**Chord**
- Straight-line distance from luff to leech
- Reference for draft measurement
- Varies along sail height

### Sail Trim Indicators

**Telltales (Woollies)**
- Yarn or ribbon flow indicators
- Attached to sails to show airflow
- Jib telltales:
  - Both streaming = perfect trim
  - Leeward fluttering = sheet in or head up
  - Windward fluttering = sheet out or bear away
- Leech telltales:
  - Should stream aft most of the time
  - Stalling = too much sheet/vang tension

**Masthead Fly (Windex)**
- Wind direction indicator at masthead
- Shows apparent wind angle
- Critical for upwind sailing
- Arrow or vane design

**Shroud Telltales**
- Telltales on shrouds
- Show apparent wind direction
- Backup to masthead fly
- Multiple heights show wind shear

**Leech Ribbons**
- Longer telltales on leech
- Show twist and leech behavior
- Should flow aft smoothly

### Trim Concepts by Condition

**Light Air Trim (0-8 knots)**
- Fuller sails (more draft)
- Loose outhaul
- Loose halyard/cunningham
- Loose backstay
- Minimal vang tension
- Traveler up to centerline or higher
- Ease sheets to avoid stalling

**Medium Air Trim (8-15 knots)**
- Moderate sail depth
- Moderate outhaul
- Some halyard/cunningham tension
- Moderate backstay
- Moderate vang
- Traveler at centerline
- Sheet trim for optimal angle

**Heavy Air Trim (15+ knots)**
- Flatter sails (less draft)
- Tight outhaul
- Maximum halyard/cunningham
- Tight backstay
- Strong vang tension
- Traveler down (leeward) to depower
- Consider reefing
- May ease sheets slightly to reduce heel

### Sail Shape Problems and Solutions

**Excessive Weather Helm**
- Boat wants to round up into wind
- Solutions:
  - Ease main traveler to leeward
  - Ease mainsheet slightly
  - Move draft forward with cunningham
  - Flatten mainsail with outhaul/backstay
  - Reef mainsail if overpowered

**Excessive Lee Helm**
- Boat wants to bear away from wind
- Solutions:
  - Trim mainsheet/traveler
  - Ease jib sheet
  - Add rake to mast (if adjustable)

**Hard to Point (Poor Upwind)**
- Can't sail close to wind
- Solutions:
  - Flatten sails (outhaul, backstay, cunningham)
  - Move draft forward
  - Check jib lead position
  - Trim sheets properly
  - Ensure proper twist

**Slow Speed**
- Boat not reaching target speed
- Solutions:
  - Check for proper draft depth
  - Verify draft position (40-50% typical)
  - Ensure twist is appropriate
  - Check telltales for attached flow
  - Clean bottom if needed

**Excessive Heel**
- Boat leaning too far
- Solutions:
  - Flatten sails
  - Ease traveler to leeward
  - Reef if necessary
  - Ease sheets to depower

### Jib Lead Adjustment

**Jib Lead Position**
- Forward-aft position of jib sheet blocks
- Critical for proper jib shape
- Affects both draft and twist

**Lead Too Far Forward**
- Tight leech, loose foot
- Leech telltales stall
- Foot of jib too flat
- Hard to head up

**Lead Too Far Aft**
- Loose leech, tight foot
- Upper leech twisted off
- Foot too tight
- Poor pointing

**Proper Lead Position**
- Even tension on luff
- All telltales break simultaneously
- Smooth curve from head to foot
- Leech telltales flowing

**Adjusting for Conditions**
- Forward in light air (more power low)
- Aft in heavy air (more twist aloft)
- Forward when overpowered (open upper leech)

### Reef Types and Techniques

**Slab Reefing (Jiffy Reefing)**
- Most common on modern boats
- Pulls sail down to boom in horizontal slabs
- First reef, second reef, third reef
- Process:
  1. Ease halyard to reef tack position
  2. Secure reef tack
  3. Tighten reefing line at clew
  4. Re-tension halyard
  5. Secure bunt of sail with reef ties

**In-Mast Furling Reef**
- Roll sail into mast partially
- Convenient but compromises shape
- No boom height reduction

**In-Boom Furling Reef**
- Roll sail into boom partially
- Better shape than in-mast
- Maintains some efficiency

## Points of Sail

### Overview

**Definition**
- Points of sail describe the boat's direction relative to the wind
- Determines sail trim and boat performance
- Critical for sailing strategy and tactics

### The No-Go Zone

**Definition**
- Range of directions directly into the wind where sailing craft cannot sail
- Typically 45° either side of true wind direction (90° total)
- Sails cannot generate lift in this zone
- Boat will stall or be "in irons"

### Points of Sail (from closest to wind to farthest)

#### 1. Close-Hauled (Beating)

**Wind Angle**: Approximately 45° off the bow
- Sailing as close to the wind as possible
- Sails trimmed tightly
- Maximum windward performance
- Also called "on the wind" or "beating"
- Beating to windward: sailing a series of close-hauled courses

**Sail Trim:**
- Sails sheeted in tight
- Minimal twist
- Luff just below point of luffing

#### 2. Close Reach

**Wind Angle**: Between 45° and 90° off the bow
- Point of sail between beating and beam reach
- Sails eased slightly from close-hauled
- Good boat speed
- Comfortable sailing angle

**Sail Trim:**
- Sheets eased from close-hauled position
- Sails still fairly flat
- Good power and speed

#### 3. Beam Reach

**Wind Angle**: 90° off the bow (perpendicular to boat)
- Wind coming directly from the side
- Often the fastest point of sail
- Most efficient use of wind power
- Easy to maintain and steer

**Sail Trim:**
- Sails approximately 45° from centerline
- Moderate twist
- Good power without excessive heel

#### 4. Broad Reach

**Wind Angle**: Between 90° and 180° off the bow (roughly 135°)
- Wind coming from behind the quarter
- Fast and comfortable
- Good for cruising
- Can use spinnaker or asymmetric

**Sail Trim:**
- Sails eased well out
- May use whisker pole for jib
- Spinnaker if available
- Watch for accidental jibe

#### 5. Running (Dead Run)

**Wind Angle**: 180° off the bow (wind directly from stern)
- Sailing directly downwind
- Wind from directly behind
- Also called "running before the wind"
- Slowest point of sail (paradoxically)

**Sail Trim:**
- Mainsail fully out to one side
- Jib can be "wing-on-wing" (opposite side) with whisker pole
- Spinnaker ideal if available
- Risk of accidental jibe

## Sailing Maneuvers and Techniques

### Basic Course Changes

#### Tacking (Coming About)

**Definition**
- Changing from one tack to the other by turning bow through the wind
- Also called "going about" or "coming about"
- Bow crosses the wind (through no-go zone)
- Sails back on opposite side

**Standard Tacking Procedure:**
1. **"Ready about"** or **"Ready to tack"** - alert crew
2. Crew prepares jib sheets (one to release, one to trim)
3. **"Helm's alee"** or **"Hard alee"** or **"Tacking"** - begin turn
4. Helmsman pushes tiller to leeward (or turns wheel to windward)
5. Release working jib sheet as bow approaches wind
6. Boat turns through eye of wind
7. Trim jib sheet on new side as bow passes through wind
8. Ease turn and settle on new close-hauled course
9. Trim main if needed
10. Check speed and pointing

**When to Tack:**
- To make progress upwind (beating to windward)
- To change direction when close-hauled
- To avoid obstacles, lee shore, or other vessels
- Strategic racing moves (covering, layline approach)
- When lifted by wind shift

**Tacking Variations:**

**Roll Tack**
- Performance tacking technique
- Crew weight moves to leeward during tack
- Then quickly to windward on new tack
- Creates rolling motion that accelerates boat
- Used in racing, especially light air

**Slam Tack**
- Aggressive, fast tack
- Quick helm movement
- Used in racing to minimize time in irons
- Requires good boat speed

**Slow Tack**
- Gradual turn through wind
- Better for heavy boats or short-handed sailing
- Less likely to get stuck in irons
- May lose more speed

**Tacking Problems:**

**Stuck in Irons**
- Boat stalls pointing into wind
- No forward motion, can't complete tack
- Solutions:
  - Back the jib to push bow off
  - Reverse engine briefly if available
  - Wait for boat to drift backward, then push tiller to desired side

**Slow Tack/Lost Speed**
- Insufficient speed before tacking
- Solution: Build speed on close-hauled before initiating tack

#### Jibing (Gybing)

**Definition**
- Turning stern through the wind
- Opposite maneuver to tacking
- Stern crosses the wind
- Boom swings from one side to other
- More dangerous than tacking due to boom swing and potential broach

**Controlled Jibe Procedure:**
1. **"Prepare to jibe"** or **"Ready to jibe"** - alert crew
2. Check area is clear for boom swing
3. Crew takes positions and prepares sheets
4. Ease jib sheet slightly
5. Sheet in mainsail toward centerline (critical safety step)
6. **"Jibe-ho"** or **"Jibe away"** - begin turn
7. Helmsman turns stern through wind
8. Boom reaches centerline
9. Boom swings across to new side (controlled by sheeted-in mainsheet)
10. Ease mainsheet quickly on new side
11. Trim jib to new side
12. Settle on new course
13. Ease main to proper trim for new point of sail

**Jibing Caution:**
- Boom swings with considerable force
- Risk of head injury - everyone must duck or be clear
- Can cause broach in strong winds or waves
- Accidental jibe extremely dangerous
- Potential for broken gear (boom, vang, traveler)
- Knockdown risk in heavy air

**Jibing Variations:**

**Dip-Pole Jibe (Spinnaker)**
- Traditional symmetric spinnaker jibe
- Pole dips below forestay during jibe
- Complex maneuver requiring crew coordination

**End-for-End Jibe (Spinnaker)**
- Pole disconnected and reversed
- Used when pole can't dip under forestay
- Common on smaller boats

**Gybe (or Flop) Jibe**
- Boom simply allowed to swing across
- Dangerous, uncontrolled
- Should be avoided

**Accidental Jibe**
- Unplanned jibe, usually from:
  - Sailing by the lee (wind on same side as boom)
  - Rolling motion in waves
  - Inattention to course/wind direction
- Prevention:
  - Watch for wind on same side as boom
  - Use preventer when running
  - Stay alert downwind

### Advanced Maneuvers

#### Heaving To

**Definition**
- Stopping or nearly stopping boat while remaining at sea
- Boat maintains position relative to wind and waves
- Self-tending configuration requires minimal crew attention
- Used for rest, repairs, meals, heavy weather riding

**Heaving To Procedure:**
1. Tack boat but DON'T release jib sheet
2. Jib backs (pushes bow away from wind)
3. Ease mainsheet significantly
4. Lash tiller/wheel to leeward (toward backed jib)
5. Boat settles at 45-50° to wind
6. Slow forward motion or drifts slowly to leeward
7. Creates slick to windward that calms seas

**When to Heave To:**
- Heavy weather survival tactic
- Rest crew during long passage
- Stop for repairs or adjustments
- Wait for daylight or tide change
- Reef or change sails in safer position
- Man overboard recovery (debated technique)

**Advantages:**
- Minimal crew effort required
- Boat self-stabilizes
- Comfortable motion in rough seas
- Creates wind shadow to leeward

#### Luffing Up (Heading Up)

**Definition**
- Turning bow toward the wind
- Decreasing angle between heading and wind
- Sails begin to luff (flap) at leading edge
- Opposite of bearing away

**When to Luff Up:**
- Slow down or depower
- Avoid obstacle to leeward
- Prepare for tacking
- Tactical racing (defend position)
- Spill wind in overpowering conditions

**Luffing (Defensive Racing Tactic)**
- Windward boat forces leeward boat to head up
- Leeward boat must respond to avoid contact
- Can force leeward boat past close-hauled (head to wind)
- Must be done properly under racing rules

#### Bearing Away (Bearing Off)

**Definition**
- Turning stern toward the wind
- Increasing angle between heading and wind
- Sails fill with more power
- Opposite of luffing up

**When to Bear Away:**
- Increase speed
- Change to reaching or running course
- Avoid obstacle to windward
- Response to being overpowered close-hauled
- Build speed before tacking

**Bearing Away from Close-Hauled:**
- Ease sheets as you bear away
- Prevents overpowering or excessive heel
- Maintain sail efficiency on new point of sail

#### Broaching

**Definition**
- Sudden, uncontrolled rounding up into wind
- Typically occurs on downwind courses
- Boat heels severely to leeward
- Rudder loses effectiveness
- Can lead to knockdown or capsize

**Causes of Broaching:**
- Excessive sail area downwind
- Large following waves overtaking boat
- Death roll (rhythmic rolling) leading to loss of control
- Too much weather helm
- Traveling too fast down waves

**Preventing Broach:**
- Reduce sail area before overpowered
- Maintain proper weight distribution
- Steer carefully down large waves
- Use boom preventer
- Reef earlier rather than later

**Recovery from Broach:**
- Release mainsheet immediately
- Steer hard to bear away (if possible)
- Crew weight to windward
- Reduce sail area once stable

#### Quick-Stop Man Overboard Recovery

**Procedure:**
1. Shout "Man overboard!" and point at person
2. Throw flotation devices (life ring, cushions)
3. Assign spotter to keep eyes on person
4. Immediately turn into wind (quick stop)
5. Let jib luff, keep mainsail centered
6. Boat slows and stops nearby
7. Circle back keeping person in sight
8. Approach from downwind
9. Stop boat with person alongside
10. Retrieve person

**Other MOB Maneuvers:**

**Figure-8 Method**
- Immediate beam reach away from person
- Tack when well past
- Return on reciprocal course
- Approach from downwind

**Williamson Turn**
- Continue course briefly
- Turn toward side person fell from
- When heading ~60° from original, continue turn
- Returns boat along reciprocal of original track

### Sail Handling Techniques

#### Setting Sails

**Hoisting Mainsail:**
1. Head boat into wind
2. Ensure halyard is clear
3. Attach halyard to head
4. Take up slack
5. Hoist while feeding luff into mast track
6. Watch for snags or twist
7. Hoist to full position (black bands or max)
8. Cleat halyard securely
9. Coil or stow excess line
10. Trim for conditions

**Setting Jib/Genoa:**
1. Head boat into wind or close-hauled
2. Attach tack to bow fitting
3. Attach sheets to clew
4. Lead sheets through blocks/fairleads
5. Attach halyard to head
6. Hoist while feeding luff onto forestay
7. Tension halyard appropriately
8. Trim sheet for point of sail

#### Reefing Under Sail

**When to Reef:**
- Before you think you need to (golden rule)
- When overpowered and uncomfortable
- When excessive weather helm develops
- When unable to depower with sheet/traveler
- When forecast indicates increasing wind
- "If you're thinking about reefing, reef"

**Slab Reef Procedure (Under Sail):**
1. Head up slightly toward wind (ease sheets)
2. Ease mainsheet and traveler
3. Ease halyard to reef tack position
4. Hook reef tack to ram's horn or gooseneck
5. Tighten reef clew line (outhaul)
6. Re-tension halyard (less than full hoist)
7. Secure excess sail with reef ties
8. Trim for new sail plan
9. Adjust jib if needed for balance

**Shaking Out Reef (Removing Reef):**
1. Head up toward wind
2. Ease mainsheet
3. Remove reef ties
4. Release reef clew line
5. Hoist halyard to full position
6. Tension outhaul
7. Return to course and trim

## Racing Terminology

### Starting and Course Terms

**Starting Line**
- Imaginary line between two marks
- Typically: committee boat and pin end buoy
- Must be crossed in correct direction at start signal
- Favored end: closer to windward mark or better wind

**Pin End**
- Buoy marking leeward end of start line
- Often port end of line
- May be favored or unfavored depending on wind/course

**Committee Boat End**
- Boat marking windward end of start line
- Usually starboard end of line
- Displays visual signals and flags

**Starting Sequence**
- Countdown to race start
- Typical sequence:
  - 5 minutes: Warning signal (class flag up)
  - 4 minutes: Preparatory signal (P, I, U, or black flag)
  - 1 minute: Preparatory flag down
  - 0 minutes: Start (class flag down, sound signal)

**Port Tack Start**
- Approaching line on port tack
- Must keep clear of starboard tack boats
- Risky but can access favored pin end

**Starboard Tack Start**
- Approaching line on starboard tack
- Right of way over port tack boats
- Safer, more common approach

**Windward Mark**
- First mark in most courses
- Requires beating (sailing upwind)
- Mark rounding crucial for position

**Leeward Mark (Offset Mark)**
- Downwind mark
- Often requires jibe to round
- May have offset mark for better approach

**Weather Mark**
- Upwind mark (same as windward mark)
- Requires beating to reach

**Reaching Mark**
- Mark on reaching leg of course
- Sailed to with wind abeam

### Tactical Racing Terms

**Layline**
- Course that leads directly to mark when close-hauled
- Port layline: approach mark on port tack
- Starboard layline: approach mark on starboard tack
- Overstating layline = extra distance sailed
- Critical for optimal mark approach

**Covering**
- Staying between competitor and next mark
- Controlling opponent's wind
- Tactical position to maintain lead
- "Loose cover": staying ahead without tight positioning
- "Tight cover": close positioning to control opponent

**Tacking Duel**
- Series of tacks between competing boats
- Typically between leader and follower
- Follower tries to break cover
- Leader maintains position
- Continues until advantage gained or course change

**Lee-Bow Position**
- Boat ahead and to leeward of opponent
- Creates bad air for trailing boat
- Forces trailing boat to tack away or slow down
- Effective tactical position

**Safe Leeward Position**
- Position ahead and to leeward
- Clear air maintained
- Right of way as leeward boat
- Opponent cannot luff without penalty

**Dirty Air (Bad Air)**
- Disturbed wind from another boat's sails
- Reduces speed and pointing ability
- Wind shadow: directly downwind of another boat
- Backwinded: turbulent air from leeward boat's sails

**Clear Air**
- Undisturbed wind
- Free from other boats' wind shadows
- Essential for optimal boat speed
- Primary goal when falling behind

**Lift**
- Wind shift allowing boat to head higher (toward mark)
- Favorable shift
- Allows sailing more direct course to windward mark
- Should tack on lifts when beating

**Header (Knock)**
- Wind shift forcing boat to head lower (away from mark)
- Unfavorable shift on that tack
- Favorable on opposite tack
- Should tack on headers when beating

**Persistent Shift**
- Wind direction change that lasts
- Requires strategy adjustment
- Different from oscillating shifts

**Oscillating Shift**
- Wind shifts back and forth
- Predictable pattern
- Tack on headers, continue on lifts
- Key to successful upwind racing

### Mark Rounding Terms

**Inside Overlap**
- Boat overlapped on inside at zone entry
- Entitled to room to round mark
- Must be established before zone (3 boat lengths)
- Key right-of-way situation

**Outside Boat**
- Boat on outside of turn at mark
- Must give room if overlap exists at zone
- Obligation to avoid contact

**Zone**
- Area around mark where mark-rounding rules apply
- Typically 3 boat lengths from mark
- Overlaps must exist at zone entry to claim room

**Mark Room**
- Space needed to round mark safely
- Inside boat entitled if overlapped at zone
- Includes room to sail proper course around mark

**Two Lengths to Go**
- Traditional call at 2 boat lengths from mark
- Historically when overlap rights established
- Now 3 lengths (zone) under current rules

**Proper Course**
- Course boat would sail to finish quickly
- Without other boats present
- Reference for rule interpretations
- Cannot sail above proper course when leeward boat

### Penalty and Protest Terms

**Protest Flag**
- Red flag displayed to indicate rule violation
- Must be displayed promptly after incident
- Hailed "Protest" to other boat
- Leads to protest hearing if not resolved

**Protest Hearing**
- Formal hearing to resolve rule disputes
- Conducted by protest committee
- Evidence presented by both parties
- Decision made based on racing rules

**Two-Turn Penalty**
- Self-imposed penalty for rule violation
- Two complete tacks and two complete jibes
- Must include one tack and one jibe
- Clears boat from protest
- Must be done promptly

**One-Turn Penalty**
- Reduced penalty in some racing formats
- One complete tack and one complete jibe
- Used in less formal racing

**720° Penalty**
- Same as two-turn penalty
- Two full circles (720 degrees)
- Includes tacks and jibes

**DSQ (Disqualification)**
- Penalty removing boat from race results
- Result of protest hearing
- Serious rule violations
- Boat scored last or worse

**RAF (Retired After Finish)**
- Boat retires after finishing race
- Accepts penalty voluntarily
- No score for that race

**DNF (Did Not Finish)**
- Boat started but didn't finish
- No score for race

**DNS (Did Not Start)**
- Boat registered but didn't start
- No score for race

**OCS (On Course Side)**
- Boat over start line early
- Must return and restart correctly
- Penalty if not corrected

### Strategy and Tactics Terms

**Favored End**
- Starting line end closer to windward mark
- Or end with better wind angle
- Determined by wind direction and course
- Start near favored end for advantage

**Playing the Shifts**
- Tactical response to wind shifts
- Tack on headers, sail on lifts
- Maximizes windward progress
- Essential upwind strategy

**Split Tacks**
- Boats on opposite tacks moving apart
- Covers different sides of course
- Hedges against wind shifts
- Risky strategy (one side usually better)

**Sailing to the Corners**
- Going far to laylines
- Maximum separation from fleet
- High risk, high reward
- Can gain or lose significantly

**Playing the Middle**
- Staying near course centerline
- Conservative strategy
- Maintains options
- Less risk than corners

**Current Strategy**
- Sailing toward favorable current
- Avoiding unfavorable current
- Can override wind strategy
- Critical in tidal waters

**Luffing Rights**
- Leeward boat's right to head up
- Before overlapped boat gets mast abeam
- Can force windward boat to respond
- Defensive tactic

**Bearing Away to Build Speed**
- Sailing slightly lower to increase speed
- Then pointing higher with momentum
- Effective in waves or light air
- "Footing" vs "pinching"

**Pinching**
- Sailing too close to wind
- Sacrifices speed for pointing
- Often slower to windward mark
- Common mistake

**Footing**
- Sailing slightly lower for speed
- May be faster to mark overall
- Better boat speed
- Depends on conditions

### Performance Metrics

**VMG (Velocity Made Good)**
- See [Sailing Performance Terms](#sailing-performance-terms)

**Boat Speed**
- Speed through water
- Independent of direction
- Measured by knotmeter

**Target Speed**
- Optimal speed for conditions
- From polar diagrams
- Performance benchmark

**Pointing Ability**
- How close to wind boat can sail
- While maintaining speed
- Varies by boat and conditions

## Sailing Performance Terms

### Speed and Course Analysis

**VMG (Velocity Made Good)**
- Speed component toward destination
- Not total boat speed
- Calculated: Boat Speed × cos(angle to destination)
- Example: 6 knots at 45° = 4.24 knots VMG
- Critical metric for upwind and downwind sailing
- Better VMG often more important than higher boat speed

**VMG Upwind**
- Component of boat speed toward windward mark
- Balance between pointing and speed
- Too close = slow speed, poor VMG
- Too low = good speed, poor VMG
- Optimal angle maximizes VMG

**VMG Downwind**
- Component of boat speed toward leeward mark
- Often faster to reach than run dead downwind
- Sailing lower angle may increase speed more than VMG loss
- Optimal angle depends on boat polar performance

**Target Boat Speed**
- Optimal speed for given wind and angle
- Derived from polar diagrams
- Performance benchmark
- Adjust trim to achieve target

**Polar Diagram (Performance Polar)**
- Graph showing boat speed potential
- Plots speed vs. wind angle for various wind speeds
- Shows optimal angles and speeds
- Used to determine target performance
- Essential tool for performance sailing

**Polar Plot Elements:**
- True wind angle (degrees from bow)
- Boat speed (knots)
- Multiple curves for different wind speeds
- VMG angles marked
- Optimal upwind and downwind angles shown

### Performance Metrics

**SOG (Speed Over Ground)**
- Actual speed relative to earth
- Includes current effect
- From GPS
- Different from speed through water (STW)

**STW (Speed Through Water)**
- Speed relative to water
- From knotmeter/paddlewheel
- Sailing performance metric
- Not affected by current

**COG (Course Over Ground)**
- Actual course made good relative to earth
- From GPS
- Includes leeway and current effects

**HDG (Heading)**
- Direction bow points
- From compass
- May differ from COG due to leeway/current

**Leeway**
- Sideways drift from wind pressure on sails
- Angle between heading and course through water
- Varies by point of sail:
  - Close-hauled: 3-10° typical
  - Reaching: 1-5°
  - Running: minimal
- Keels and daggerboards reduce leeway

**Leeway Angle**
- Measured angle of sideways drift
- Difference between heading and course through water
- More leeway in light air or shallow draft boats

**Set and Drift (Current)**
- Set: Direction current flows (toward)
- Drift: Speed of current (knots)
- Must be calculated into course planning
- Affects COG and SOG

### Boat Attitude and Motion

**Angle of Heel**
- Tilt angle from vertical
- Measured in degrees
- Optimal heel varies by boat:
  - Modern racers: 15-25° upwind
  - Cruisers: 10-20° comfortable
  - Excessive heel reduces performance
- Controlled by sail trim and crew weight

**Pitch**
- Fore-and-aft rocking motion
- Bow rises and falls
- Excessive pitching slows boat
- Caused by waves or improper weight distribution

**Hobby-Horsing**
- Excessive pitching motion
- Bow rises then slams into waves
- Very slow and uncomfortable
- Caused by:
  - Too much weight in ends
  - Wrong trim
  - Improper sailing technique in waves

**Pounding**
- Hull slamming into waves
- Usually at bow
- Uncomfortable and slow
- Can damage boat
- Bear away or reduce speed to minimize

**Weather Helm**
- Tendency to round up into wind
- Tiller/wheel pressure toward weather (windward) side
- Small amount desirable (5-7° rudder angle)
- Excessive causes drag and slow speed
- Causes:
  - Too much mainsail area
  - Too little headsail
  - Mast rake aft
  - Hull imbalance

**Lee Helm**
- Tendency to bear away from wind
- Tiller/wheel pressure toward lee (leeward) side
- Generally undesirable
- Harder to control boat
- Feels "loose"
- Causes:
  - Too little mainsail
  - Too much headsail
  - Mast rake forward

**Neutral Helm**
- No tendency to round up or bear away
- Light tiller pressure
- Boat tracks straight
- Ideal for some conditions
- Some weather helm usually preferred for feel

### Wave and Sea Performance

**Surfing**
- Riding down face of wave
- Boat accelerates significantly
- Speed can exceed hull speed
- Thrilling but requires control
- Common on broad reach or run in waves

**Hull Speed**
- Theoretical maximum displacement hull speed
- Formula: 1.34 × √(LWL in feet) = speed in knots
- Example: 30' LWL = 1.34 × √30 = 7.3 knots
- Can be exceeded:
  - Surfing
  - Planing (light boats)
  - Semi-displacement hulls

**Planing**
- Hull rises and rides on top of water
- Exceeds displacement hull speed
- Requires:
  - Light displacement
  - Flat aft sections
  - Sufficient power (wind or engine)
  - Proper trim
- Dinghies and performance boats plane easily
- Heavy cruisers rarely plane

**Displacement Mode**
- Hull pushes through water
- Speed limited by hull speed
- Most cruising boats operate in displacement mode
- Wave created by hull limits speed

**Semi-Displacement**
- Transitional mode between displacement and planing
- Moderate speed increase possible
- Some lift from hull shape
- Modern performance cruisers designed for this

### Performance Optimization

**Sail Area to Displacement Ratio (SA/D)**
- Measure of boat's power
- Higher ratio = more performance potential
- Formula: SA / (displacement in tons)^(2/3)
- Typical values:
  - Under 15: underpowered
  - 15-18: moderate cruiser
  - 18-22: performance cruiser
  - Over 22: racing boat

**Displacement to Length Ratio (D/L)**
- Indicates how heavy boat is for its length
- Formula: (displacement in tons / (0.01 × LWL)^3)
- Typical values:
  - Under 100: ultralight
  - 100-200: light
  - 200-300: moderate
  - 300-400: heavy
  - Over 400: very heavy
- Affects performance, comfort, carrying capacity

**Motion Comfort Ratio**
- Predicts boat motion in waves
- Higher number = more comfortable
- Formula includes displacement, beam, LWL
- Typical values:
  - Under 20: uncomfortable
  - 20-30: average
  - 30-40: comfortable
  - Over 40: very comfortable

**Capsize Screening Formula**
- Indicates capsize risk
- Formula: Beam / (displacement in pounds / 64)^(1/3)
- Values:
  - Under 2.0: suitable for offshore
  - Over 2.0: increasing risk offshore
  - Over 2.5: coastal or protected waters

### Instrument Terms

**True Wind Speed (TWS)**
- Actual wind speed
- What you'd feel standing still
- From instruments calculating from apparent wind

**True Wind Angle (TWA)**
- Angle of true wind to boat heading
- Calculated from apparent wind and boat speed
- Used for polar performance analysis

**Apparent Wind Speed (AWS)**
- Wind speed felt on moving boat
- Combination of true wind and boat motion
- What instruments measure directly
- What sails respond to

**Apparent Wind Angle (AWA)**
- Angle of apparent wind to boat heading
- Measured by masthead instruments
- Used for sail trim reference
- Always forward of true wind when moving

**Wind Gradient**
- Wind speed increase with height
- Wind aloft stronger than at surface
- Causes sail twist to be efficient
- Reason for twist in sail trim

### Performance Analysis Terms

**Optimal Angle**
- Best course angle for conditions
- Upwind: balance pointing vs. speed
- Downwind: may be reach, not dead run
- From polar diagrams

**Speed Loss in Tack**
- Speed reduction during tacking
- Modern boats: 20-40% speed loss
- Heavy boats: more loss
- Quick recovery important

**Speed Loss in Jibe**
- Speed reduction during jibing
- Usually less than tacking
- Controlled jibe minimizes loss

**Acceleration**
- How quickly boat builds speed
- Light boats accelerate faster
- Important after tacks or in lulls
- Affected by displacement, sail area

**Deceleration (Drag)**
- How quickly boat loses speed
- Light boats decelerate faster
- Heavy boats carry momentum better
- Factors:
  - Hull shape
  - Wetted surface
  - Weight
  - Wave impact

## Crew Commands and Communication

### Standard Helm Commands

**"Ready About" / "Ready to Tack"**
- Helmsman announces intention to tack
- Crew prepares jib sheets
- Response: "Ready" when crew prepared
- Not an execution command

**"Helm's Alee" / "Hard Alee" / "Tacking"**
- Execution command for tacking
- Helmsman pushes tiller to leeward
- Crew releases jib sheet
- Immediate action required

**"Prepare to Jibe" / "Ready to Jibe"**
- Helmsman announces intention to jibe
- Crew prepares for boom swing
- All hands take safe positions
- Mainsail sheeted toward center

**"Jibe-Ho" / "Jibe Away"**
- Execution command for jibing
- Helmsman turns stern through wind
- Crew controls boom swing
- Everyone ducks or clears boom path

**"Ease the Sheet"**
- Let out sail control line
- Sails out, less power
- Specified sheet (main or jib)

**"Trim" / "Sheet In" / "Trim In"**
- Pull in sail control line
- Sails in, more power or pointing
- Specified sheet (main or jib)

**"Hard Over"**
- Turn wheel/tiller to maximum
- Emergency or sharp turn
- Often with direction: "Hard to starboard"

**"Ease the Helm" / "Come Up"**
- Turn toward wind (luff up)
- Decrease turning pressure

**"Bear Away" / "Bear Off" / "Head Down"**
- Turn away from wind
- Increase angle to wind
- Prepare to ease sheets

### Sail Handling Commands

**"Hoist Away" / "Hoist the Main"**
- Begin raising specified sail
- Halyard crew pulls halyard
- Continues until "that's good" or sail fully hoisted

**"Avast" / "That's Good" / "Stop"**
- Cease current action
- Stop hoisting, easing, or trimming
- Hold current position

**"Belay" / "Make Fast"**
- Secure line to cleat
- Stop and tie off
- Line under control and cleated

**"Two-Block" / "Sweat It Up"**
- Hoist to maximum position
- Get every last inch of tension
- Blocks touching (two-blocked)

**"Douse" / "Drop" / "Lower Away"**
- Lower specified sail
- Control descent
- Prepare to secure

**"Ease the Halyard"**
- Let out halyard slightly
- Lower sail to specific position (reef point)
- Control tension reduction

**"Set the Pole"**
- Deploy whisker pole or spinnaker pole
- Prepare for downwind sailing
- Specific sail setup command

**"Take in the Slack"**
- Remove loose line
- Prepare for trimming or hoisting
- Pre-tension command

**"Cast Off"**
- Release specified line
- Let go of dock line or sheet
- Preparation for maneuver

### Situational Awareness Commands

**"Starboard!"**
- Racing call indicating right-of-way
- Approaching boat is on starboard tack
- Other boat must give way
- Loudly hailed to port-tack boat

**"Water!" / "Room!"**
- Request for space or right-of-way
- At mark roundings or obstructions
- Inside boat calling for mark room

**"Clear Ahead"**
- Boat has established lead
- No overlap exists
- Racing position call

**"Overlap" / "Inside"**
- Boats side-by-side
- Claiming inside position
- Mark rounding rights established

**"Protest!"**
- Racing rule violation claim
- Must display protest flag promptly
- Hailed to offending boat

**"Duck!" / "Heads Up!" / "Boom!"**
- Warning of boom swing
- Usually during jibe
- Safety alert - immediate response required

**"Watch Your Head"**
- Boom or other gear swinging
- Preventive safety warning

**"Trim the Jib"**
- Pull in jib sheet
- Increase jib power or pointing

**"Ease the Jib"**
- Let out jib sheet
- Reduce power or adjust for point of sail

### Emergency Commands

**"Man Overboard!" / "MOB!"**
- Person in water emergency
- All hands respond
- Immediate action protocol initiated
- Continuous pointing at person in water

**"Crew Overboard - Port/Starboard Side!"**
- Specifies which side person fell
- Helps with recovery approach
- Spotter assigned to that side

**"All Hands on Deck!"**
- Emergency situation
- Everyone needed immediately
- Leave all other tasks

**"Stand By to Drop Anchor!"**
- Prepare ground tackle
- Approach to anchorage
- Or emergency anchoring preparation

**"Let Go!" (Anchoring)**
- Release anchor
- Drop anchor command
- Begin paying out rode

**"Heave To!"**
- Execute heaving to maneuver
- Prepare for stopping boat
- Heavy weather or emergency procedure

### Anchoring Commands

**"Stand By the Anchor"**
- Prepare to drop anchor
- Approach to anchorage
- Bow crew ready at windlass

**"Let Go" / "Drop the Anchor"**
- Release anchor
- Begin paying out rode
- Anchoring execution

**"Pay Out Scope"**
- Let out more anchor rode
- Increase holding power
- Specified amount or ratio

**"Hold" / "Snub"**
- Stop paying out rode
- Test anchor set
- Check dragging

**"Anchor's Set"**
- Anchor holding
- Set properly on bottom
- Reverse test complete

**"We're Dragging"**
- Anchor not holding
- Boat moving from position
- Re-anchor required

**"Up and Down"**
- Rode vertical during retrieval
- Anchor directly below
- Nearly at surface

**"Anchor's Aweigh"**
- Anchor free of bottom
- Still underwater but unhooked
- Can begin maneuvering

**"Anchor's Aboard"**
- Anchor on deck and secured
- Ready for passage
- Windlass operations complete

### General Sailing Commands

**"Stand By"**
- Prepare for action
- Alert command
- Specific action to follow

**"Clear"**
- Safe to proceed
- No obstruction
- Area or situation secure

**"Mark"**
- Record current position or reading
- GPS waypoint or instrument reading
- Often for navigation

**"Hold Your Course"**
- Maintain current heading
- Don't alter course
- During sail trim or crew movement

**"Steady As She Goes"**
- Maintain current heading
- Keep this course
- Heading is satisfactory

**"Come to Heading [XXX]"**
- Turn to specified compass course
- Navigate to specific heading
- Usually degrees magnetic or true

**"Fall Off" / "Head Down"**
- Turn away from wind
- Bear away
- Increase angle to wind

**"Come Up" / "Head Up"**
- Turn toward wind
- Luff up
- Decrease angle to wind

### Docking Commands

**"Prepare Fenders"**
- Deploy fenders on appropriate side
- Protect hull for docking
- Port or starboard specified

**"Prepare Dock Lines"**
- Ready bow, stern, and spring lines
- Coil and prepare for throwing
- Specific lines called out

**"Port Side To" / "Starboard Side To"**
- Which side will contact dock
- Determines fender and line placement

**"Bow Line Ashore"**
- Forward line secured to dock
- First line usually
- Prevents boat moving forward

**"Stern Line Ashore"**
- Aft line secured to dock
- Prevents boat moving backward
- Usually second line

**"Spring Lines Ashore"**
- Forward and aft spring lines secured
- Prevents boat moving fore/aft
- Complete docking setup

**"Secure All Lines"**
- Tighten and adjust all dock lines
- Final docking positions
- Fenders adjusted

**"Cast Off All Lines"**
- Release all dock lines from shore
- Departure preparation
- In specific order if requested

### Communication Protocols

**Call and Response**
- Helmsman: "Ready about"

- Confirms crew prepared
- Safety communication

**Repeat Back Critical Information**

**"On It" / "Doing It"**

**"Done" / "Complete" / "Made"**

- Ready for next command

## Wind-Related Terms

### Primary Wind Terms

**Windward**
- Upwind from the point of reference
- Toward the direction from which wind is coming
- Also called "weather side" (historical/traditional)
- The side of ship facing the wind

**Leeward** (pronounced "loo-erd")
- Downwind from the point of reference
- Along the direction toward which wind is going
- The "lee side" of a ship
- Protected from wind

**Key Difference:**
- Windward = where wind comes FROM
- Leeward = where wind goes TO

### Wind Characteristics by Location

**Windward Side:**
- Wind usually stronger
- More constant
- Direct wind exposure
- Higher wind speed

**Leeward Side:**
- More variable wind
- Weaker gusts
- Wind shadow effect
- Protected area

### Wind-Related Hazards

**Lee Shore**
- Shore on the leeward side of vessel
- Wind blows vessel toward shore
- Dangerous situation
- Difficult to sail away from
- Risk of running aground

**Weather Shore**
- Shore on the windward side of vessel
- Wind blows vessel away from shore
- Safer situation
- Easier to manage

### Sailing Rules Related to Wind

**Right-of-Way Between Sailing Vessels (COLREGS Rule 12):**

1. **Different tacks:**
   - Vessel on port tack gives way to vessel on starboard tack

2. **Same tack:**
   - Windward vessel gives way to leeward vessel
   - Windward vessel is more maneuverable

3. **Port tack vessel uncertain:**
   - Shall give way to other vessel

**Rationale:**
- Windward vessel is normally more maneuverable
- Has clearer view of leeward vessel
- Wind gives better control to windward vessel

### Additional Wind Terms

**True Wind**
- Actual wind speed and direction
- Wind experienced when stationary
- What weather stations measure

**Apparent Wind**
- Wind felt on moving boat
- Combination of true wind and boat's motion
- What sails respond to
- Always forward of true wind when moving

**Wind Shift**
- Change in wind direction
- Header: wind shifts toward bow (forces you to bear away)
- Lift: wind shifts toward stern (allows you to head up)

**Wind Shadow**
- Area of reduced wind behind obstacle
- Created by sails, land, or other vessels
- Affects performance in racing

## Tide and Current Terms

### Tidal Movement Terms

**Flood Tide / Flood Current**
- Rising tide
- Change from low tide to high tide
- Incoming tide along coast and into bays and estuaries
- Water level increasing

**Ebb Tide / Ebb Current**
- Falling tide
- Change from high tide to low tide
- Outgoing tide from bays and estuaries
- Water level decreasing

**Slack Water / Slack Tide**
- Moment when tidal current ceases
- Speed of tidal current is very weak or zero
- Period of reversal between ebb and flood currents
- Usually brief interval
- Best time for anchoring or navigating narrow channels

### Tidal Range and Types

**Tidal Range**
- Difference in height between high tide and low tide
- Varies by location and lunar cycle
- Important for navigation and anchoring

**High Tide (High Water)**
- Maximum water level during tidal cycle
- Occurs approximately twice daily (in most locations)
- Timing advances ~50 minutes each day

**Low Tide (Low Water)**
- Minimum water level during tidal cycle
- Occurs approximately twice daily (in most locations)
- Timing advances ~50 minutes each day

### Lunar-Influenced Tide Types

**Spring Tides**
- Occur during full moon and new moon
- Earth, Sun, and Moon are aligned
- Combined tidal effect is greatest
- Largest difference between high and low tide
- Highest high tides and lowest low tides
- Occur twice each lunar month, year-round
- Not related to the season "spring"

**Neap Tides**
- Occur during first and third quarter moons
- Earth, Sun, and Moon form right angle
- Combined tidal effect is least
- Smallest difference between high and low tide
- Moderate tides:
  - High tides are lower than average
  - Low tides are higher than average
- Occur twice each lunar month

### Tidal Predictions

**Tidal Height Datum**
- Reference level for tide predictions
- Common datums:
  - Mean Lower Low Water (MLLW)
  - Mean Low Water (MLW)
  - Chart Datum

**Tide Tables**
- Predict times and heights of tides
- Specific to location
- Published annually
- Available in print and digital formats

### Current Terms

**Tidal Current**
- Horizontal movement of water due to tides
- Different from tidal height
- Can be strong in narrow channels
- Affects vessel speed and course

**Set**
- Direction toward which current flows
- Expressed in degrees true

**Drift**
- Speed of current
- Expressed in knots

**Current Navigation**
- Must account for set and drift
- Affects course made good
- Important for accurate navigation
- Use current tables and charts

### Tidal Navigation Considerations

**Rising Tide (Flood):**
- Allows access to shallow areas
- Easier to approach shore
- Can help lift grounded vessel

**Falling Tide (Ebb):**
- Risk of grounding in shallow areas
- Strong currents leaving harbors
- May expose hazards

**Tide and Current Differences:**
- Tide = vertical water movement
- Current = horizontal water movement
- Current can flow strongly during slack tide (due to river flow or other factors)
- Slack tide ≠ slack current always

## Depth and Hull Terms

### Depth Measurements

**Draft (or Draught)**
- Depth of vessel below the waterline
- Measured vertically to the lowest part of the hull, propellers, or other reference point
- Minimum depth of water in which boat will float
- More heavily loaded = deeper draft
- Critical for navigation in shallow water

**Types of Draft:**
- **Forward draft**: Depth at bow
- **Aft draft**: Depth at stern
- **Maximum draft**: Deepest point of vessel

**Freeboard**
- Distance from waterline to upper deck level
- Measured at lowest point of sheer where water can enter
- Difference between depth and draft
- Vertical measure of hull above water level
- Important for seaworthiness and safety

**Relationship:**
- Freeboard = Total Depth - Draft
- More cargo/weight = less freeboard, more draft

### Hull Reference Lines

**Waterline**
- Intersection of boat's hull and water's surface
- Where boat sits in water
- Changes with loading
- Design waterline vs. actual waterline

**Load Waterline (LWL)**
- Waterline when boat is at designed load
- Used for calculating performance
- Reference for displacement calculations

**Sheer**
- Upward curve of deck from midships to bow and stern
- Aesthetic and functional design element
- Affects freeboard measurement

### Weight and Displacement

**Displacement**
- Weight of water displaced by floating vessel
- Equal to the actual weight of ship
- In mass terms, equals buoyant force provided by water
- Measured in long tons or metric tonnes

**Types of Displacement:**
- **Light displacement**: Empty vessel
- **Load displacement**: Fully loaded vessel

**Deadweight Tonnage (DWT)**
- Measure of how much weight ship can carry
- Sum of weights of:
  - Cargo
  - Fuel
  - Fresh water
  - Ballast water
  - Provisions
  - Passengers
  - Crew

**Gross Tonnage (GT)**
- Based on total volume of vessel in cubic meters
- Formula applied to internal volume
- Not a measure of weight
- Used for registration and regulations

### Other Hull Terms

**Depth**
- Vertical distance from keel to deck
- Measured at midships
- Different from draft (which is to waterline)

**Beam**
- Maximum width of vessel
- Measured at widest point
- Important for stability and capacity

**Length Overall (LOA)**
- Maximum length of vessel
- Measured from foremost to aftmost points
- Includes bowsprit, pulpit, and other extensions

**Length Waterline (LWL)**
- Length of hull at waterline
- Important for speed calculations
- Theoretical hull speed = 1.34 × √LWL (in feet)

**Trim**
- Difference between forward and aft draft
- Vessel trimmed by bow: deeper draft forward
- Vessel trimmed by stern: deeper draft aft
- Proper trim optimizes performance

**List**
- Lean to one side
- Port list or starboard list
- Caused by uneven weight distribution or damage

**Heel**
- Temporary lean caused by wind or waves
- Normal in sailing
- Returns to upright when force removed

## Additional Important Terms

### General Sailing Terms

**Making Way**
- Vessel is moving through the water
- Has steerage (can be steered)

**Underway**
- Vessel is not at anchor, made fast to shore, or aground
- May or may not be making way

**Adrift**
- Floating without control
- Not anchored or secured
- At mercy of wind and current

**Aground**
- Vessel touching or resting on the bottom
- Intentional (beaching) or unintentional (grounding)

### Watch Terms

**Watch**
- Duty period for crew members
- Typically 4-hour shifts
- Ensures 24-hour coverage

**Watch System:**
- First watch: 2000-2400
- Middle watch: 0000-0400
- Morning watch: 0400-0800
- Forenoon watch: 0800-1200
- Afternoon watch: 1200-1600
- Dog watches: 1600-1800, 1800-2000 (split to rotate schedule)

### Speed Terms

**Making Knots**
- Traveling at specific speed in knots
- "Making 6 knots" = traveling at 6 nautical miles per hour

**Full Speed**
- Maximum speed vessel can achieve

**Dead Slow**
- Slowest speed while maintaining steerage

**All Stop**
- Engine stopped, vessel coasting

### Distance Terms

**Cable**
- One-tenth of a nautical mile
- 608 feet or 185 meters
- Used for shorter distances

**League**
- Three nautical miles
- Historical distance measurement

### Safety Terms

**Lifelines**
- Wire or rope lines around deck edge
- Prevents crew from going overboard
- Supported by stanchions

**Jacklines**
- Lines running fore and aft on deck
- Crew attaches safety harness tether
- Allows movement while staying attached

**Safety Harness**
- Worn by crew in rough conditions
- Tether attaches to jacklines
- Prevents going overboard
